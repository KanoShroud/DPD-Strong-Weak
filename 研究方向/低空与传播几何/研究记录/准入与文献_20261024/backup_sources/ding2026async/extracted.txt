Abstract— Existing direct position determination (DPD) meth-
ods for moving radiation sources based on time difference of arrival
(TDOA) and frequency difference of arrival (FDOA) typically
assume synchronous sensor networks, rendering them unsuitable
for asynchronous networks affected by clock and frequency biases.
To address this limitation and extend applicability to large-scale
sensor networks, this paper proposes a distributed DPD algorithm
for moving sources in asynchronous sensor networks based on
calibration sources. To achieve distributed global optimization of
parameters including clock and frequency offsets between neigh-
boring sensors as well as the position and velocity of the source,
this study introduces a distributed multiple particle filtering (MPF)
framework. Utilizing the coarse estimates provided by the dis-
tributed MPF, the algorithm then applies a diffusion-based gradient
method to obtain the refined estimates. Throughout the entire
process, the radiation source is regarded as an increasingly reliable
calibration source. The algorithm jointly utilizes information from
the radiation source and the calibration sources to estimate clock
and frequency biases. Meanwhile, information exchange in the
algorithm is restricted to neighboring sensors, eliminating the
need for multi-hop transmission. Experimental results demonstrate
consistent convergence of the estimated parameters across sensors
and validate the superior performance of the proposed algorithm,
even under challenging conditions of low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
and limited observation duration.

I.
INTRODUCTION
Passive localization of moving radiation sources has
gained significant research attention in recent years. This
technology is a crucial function of wireless sensor net-
works, widely applied in electronic reconnaissance, en-
vironmental sensing, the Internet of Things (IoT), and
security systems [1]–[3].
To achieve high-precision localization of moving ra-
diation sources, sensor networks typically combine time
difference of arrival (TDOA) and frequency difference of
arrival (FDOA) information for localization [4]. Tradi-
tional localization approaches, known as two-step meth-
ods, perform localization in two stages. In the first stage,
the TDOA and FDOA values relative to a reference
node are estimated based on the signals received by the
sensors [5], [6]. In the second stage, these estimated
parameters are further processed to derive the target’s
position estimate. Classical data processing algorithm
linearizes the localization equations and obtains target
velocity and position estimates using a two-step weighted
least squares method [7]. Numerous other algorithms for
data processing have also been proposed in [8]–[10].
However, the two-step methods do not fully leverage the
correlation among received signals and incur information
loss during the initial estimation of parameters, leading
to significant localization errors under low signal-to-noise
ratio (SNR) environments. To address this limitation, A. J.
Weiss proposed the direct position determination (DPD)
algorithm in [11]. The DPD method bypasses the interme-
diate estimation of localization parameters such as TDOA,
while directly estimates the target position from the re-
ceived signals via a one-step signal processing procedure.
Specifically, the DPD algorithm constructs a cost function
related to the source position and searches for its extreme
point. The cost function exploits the correlation among
the received signals and is commonly constructed based
on the maximum likelihood (ML) criterion. The point
that maximizes or minimizes the cost function within
the search space corresponds to the estimate, at which
the correlation among the received signals is maximized.
Compared with two-step methods, the DPD algorithm
achieves higher localization accuracy, especially in low
SNR environments [12]–[14].
Building upon the introduced DPD algorithm, meth-
ods for direct localization or direct tracking of moving
targets using TDOA and FDOA information have been
proposed in recent decades. In [15], particle filter (PF)
was utilized to implement direct tracking of moving
targets based on TDOA and FDOA information. To enable
scalability for large-scale sensor networks, [16] proposed
a distributed particle filtering approach for distributed
direct tracking, thereby reducing the computational and
communication burdens on individual sensors. However,

both methods require multiple observations of the ra-
diation source. In practical scenarios, the source signal
may be intermittent, rendering these methods unsuit-
able for single-shot localization applications. Addressing
the single-shot localization scenario, [17] employed the
Gaussian-Newton (GN) method to iteratively estimate
the source position from a single set of measurements.
While well-suited for single-shot localization, the GN
method is highly sensitive to the initial value and may
become trapped in local optima if the initial guess is
unsuitable, resulting in large localization errors. To ad-
dress the challenge of global optimization, a multiple
particle filtering (MPF) method was introduced in [4].
This approach not only ensures global solutions but also
employs two interacting PFs that separately optimize the
source’s position and velocity, decomposing the search
space into two lower-dimensional subspaces. This division
significantly reduces the number of particles required
and lowers the computational complexity compared with
traditional PF methods.
The aforementioned algorithms assume the use of
synchronous sensor networks for direct localization of
moving targets. However, in the absence of devices such
as the global positioning system (GPS) for time syn-
chronization, clock biases commonly exist among sensors
and the sensor network is asynchronous. When localizing
targets using asynchronous sensor networks, it has been
demonstrated in [18] that uncorrected clock biases can
cause substantial localization errors. To address this, the
authors proposed a DPD algorithm based on calibration
sources. This approach first obtains initial estimates of
the target position and clock biases via the expectation-
maximization (EM) algorithm, and then refines these
estimates using the GN method. In [19], the impact of
time synchronization errors was mitigated by repeatedly
changing the sensor positions and performing multiple
observations of the target. However, both of these methods
focus exclusively on localizing stationary targets and con-
sider only TDOA information. When the frequency shifts
caused by the Doppler effect are not taken into account,
significant localization errors may occur for high-speed
moving targets. Moreover, neither approach addresses the
issue of frequency biases. In sensor networks, each sensor
uses its local oscillator to down-convert the received
signals. Due to factors such as manufacturing variations
and temperature-induced oscillator drift, frequency offsets
arise among the received baseband signals from different
sensors [20], [21]. These frequency biases can adversely
affect both position and velocity estimation of sources.
Therefore, to achieve high-precision localization, it is
essential to calibrate both the clock and frequency biases
across the sensors [22], [23].
This paper thus focuses on the problem of localizing
moving radiation sources using asynchronous sensor net-
works subject to both time and frequency synchronization
errors. To overcome the inherent drawbacks of centralized
processing, such as limited system robustness and the high
communication bandwidth and computational demands
placed on the fusion center [24]–[26], we propose a
distributed DPD algorithm. The algorithm is designed to
estimate a high-dimensional parameter formed by clock
and frequency biases as well as source’s position and
velocity. The estimation of clock and frequency biases
is achieved by leveraging calibration sources with known
positions and velocities but unknown transmitted signals.
To address the challenge of distributed global optimiza-
tion in the high dimensional parameter space, we intro-
duce a distributed MPF method based on the diffusion
strategy in [26]–[28]. By decomposing the search space
and enabling interaction among PFs as well as local
fusion among sensors, this approach effectively reduces
the required number of particles and achieves globally
consistent estimation. For further improvement of local-
ization accuracy, the output of the distributed MPF is
utilized as the initialization for subsequent processing,
which employs the diffusion strategy and the gradient
method to refine estimates toward consistent and accurate
results. Unlike the distributed DPD methods in [19],
[25], and [29], which require multi-hop transmissions and
global data fusion, the proposed algorithm only necessi-
tates information exchange between neighboring sensors,
eliminating the need for storage and forwarding. Each
sensor processes only local information, and consistent
convergence across the network is achieved through the
diffusion strategy. Consequently, the algorithm is well-
suited for application in large-scale sensor networks.
The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
• The scenario of distributed localization of moving
sources in asynchronous sensor networks with both
clock and frequency biases is modeled. Calibration
sources are utilized to correct clock and frequency
biases. For this setting, the local and global cost
functions based on the ML criterion are proposed.
The Cram´er-Rao lower bound (CRLB) for this sce-
nario is also derived, which is distinct from the
results provided in [4], [15], and [18].
• A fully distributed DPD algorithm is developed to lo-
calize moving radiation sources in asynchronous sen-
sor networks based on the constructed cost functions.
This algorithm utilizes the proposed distributed MPF
method to obtain reliable initial estimates, which
are then refined using a diffusion-based gradient
approach. All sensors in the network achieve con-
sistent convergence across the estimated parameters.
Throughout the entire process, information is ex-
changed only between neighboring sensors, and local
fusion is performed, thereby eliminating the need for
multi-hop transmission and centralized processing.
• The impact of clock and frequency biases on local-
ization is analyzed both theoretically and numeri-
cally.

II. MODELING
This section provides the mathematical modeling of
the scenario in which an asynchronous sensor network
is used to localize moving radiation sources. Since the
localization of multiple sources after signal separation can
be transformed into a series of single-source localization
problems, a single source is assumed for simplification.
The non-cooperative radiation source to be located moves
with a speed of v at a position of u, where both the
velocity and position are unknown, and the dimension
of the localization is denoted as d. The sensor network
consists of M fixed sensors distributed in space to localize
the radiation source. It is assumed that each sensor
knows its position precisely, with the position of the i-th
sensor denoted as pi. The signal emitted by the radiation
source propagates to each sensor via line-of-sight. Under
the narrowband Doppler assumption, the Doppler effect
is modeled as introducing solely a frequency shift to
the received baseband signal. After down conversion,
the baseband signal received by the i-th sensor can be
expressed as

III.
PROPOSED ALGORITHM
For DPD algorithms, the procedure begins with con-
structing a cost function, followed by searching within
the parameter space for a point that maximizes or min-
imizes this cost function. This point corresponds to the
algorithm’s estimate of the parameter. Therefore, this
section derives the cost function on which the algorithm
is based and presents the solution procedure. The solution
consists of two stages: distributed coarse estimation and
distributed fine estimation. The coarse estimation provides
a reliable initialization for the iterative refinement in the
fine estimation stage.

B. Consistent Convergence Across the Sensor
Network
To evaluate the convergence performance of the pro-
posed algorithm across sensors, the RMSE curves of the
estimated parameters at each sensor are presented in Fig.
6. In this experiment, the received SNR of the radiation
source and calibration source signals at each sensor is
set to 10 dB. As shown, during both the coarse and
fine estimation stages, the RMSE curves from sensors
converge together for every estimated parameter as the
iterations progress. These results demonstrate that the
algorithm achieves consistent convergence throughout the
sensor network. It can also be observed that the velocity
RMSE curve has a part of rising during coarse estimation
stage. This phenomenon is caused by the weighted combi-
nation of local estimates within the distributed algorithm.
During coarse estimation stage, sensors that settle near
the true value are influenced by neighbors that are still far
from the true value. The integration of these larger errors
through the neighborhood weighting mechanism causes
the estimates of the “better” sensors to deviate slightly
from the truth, resulting in the observed rise in RMSE.
Furthermore, the RMSE values at each sensor con-
tinue to decrease in the fine estimation stage, indicating
that the fine estimation step refines the estimates with
higher accuracy. Notably, the RMSE curves for clock
and frequency biases exhibit a three-level step pattern.
This phenomenon arises because the algorithm performs
synchronization in two phases during the coarse estima-
tion stage: pre-calibration and joint calibration. During
joint calibration, by further leveraging the calibration
information from the radiation source and applying the
diffusion strategy to incorporate information from the
entire network, sensors obtain more reliable estimates
of clock and frequency biases. According to the signal
model in (1), the clock and frequency bias are strongly
coupled with the position and velocity of the radiation
source, respectively. Therefore, improved coarse estimates
provide more accurate initializations for the subsequent
fine estimation step, allowing the algorithm to converge to
more precise estimates through gradient-based methods.
C.
Performance Comparison
After verifying the consistent convergence of the
proposed algorithm, this subsection compares its perfor-
mance with other methods that localize moving targets
using TDOA and FDOA information. The benchmark
algorithms include the two-step algorithm from [9], the
DPD algorithm from [4] (denoted as MPF DPD), and a
DPD algorithm using only the proposed distributed MPF
(denoted as DMPF DPD). Both the two-step algorithm
and MPF DPD are centralized algorithms, whereas the
proposed algorithm and DMPF DPD are distributed. In
the two-step algorithm, TDOAs and FDOAs are estimated
by searching for the maximum points of CAFs via grid
search (referred to as the CAF method), with grid sizes
of 15 m for TDOA (equivalent to 0.1/fs) and 100 Hz for
FDOA. In both the two-step algorithm and MPF DPD,
clock and frequency biases are also estimated by the
CAF method using calibration source information. For
a fair comparison, the PFs in MPF DPD are configured
with 100 particles and run for 100 iterations. Conse-
quently, the computational load at the fusion center in
MPF DPD is significantly higher than the computational
burden distributed across individual sensors in DMPF
DPD. The CRLBs for all estimated parameters are derived
in Appendix B.

ibration source signals at each sensor is varied from -
7.5 dB to 12.5 dB in 2.5 dB increments to compare the
estimation performance among different algorithms. As
shown in Fig. 7, the proposed algorithm achieves the

method significantly lowers communication bandwidth
and processing requirements, making it highly suitable
for large-scale sensor network applications. Although the
processing time of the proposed algorithm remains on the
order of seconds, this delay is acceptable since we are
not considering the recursive online tracking of moving
sources [4]. Moreover, the running time can be further
reduced by utilizing high-performance servers, multi-
threading techniques (e.g., assigning separate threads to
particles during coarse estimation), or low-level program-
ming languages.

The Influence of Calibration Sources
In this subsection, the impact of calibration sources on
the performance of the proposed algorithm is investigated
by varying both the number and signal strength of cali-
bration sources. The SNR of the radiation source signal
is fixed at 5 dB, while the SNR of each calibration source
signal is gradually increased from -7.5 dB to 12.5 dB. As
shown in Fig. 12 (c) and Fig. 12 (d), the estimation
performance for clock and frequency biases improves
with an increasing number of calibration sources and
higher SNRs of the calibration source signals. Since clock
bias and frequency bias are respectively coupled with the
target’s position and velocity, the accuracy of position and
velocity estimation improves as the number of calibration
sources increases, as illustrated in Fig. 12 (a) and Fig. 12
(b). This also explains why joint calibration yields more
accurate estimates compared to relying on a single cali-
bration source. When the position and velocity estimates
of the radiation source are accurate, joint calibration is
effectively equivalent to using two calibration sources.
Moreover, the results indicate that under conditions of
low calibration source SNR, increasing the number of cal-
ibration sources significantly enhances estimation perfor-
mance. However, when the SNR of the calibration sources
exceeds 10 dB, adding additional calibration sources has
little impact on localization performance. These results
validate the theoretical analysis presented in the Appendix

VI. CONCLUSION
This paper addressed the problem of direct localiza-
tion of moving sources in asynchronous sensor networks,
which are subject to both time and frequency synchro-
nization errors. A distributed algorithm that leverages
calibration sources for synchronization was proposed.
The algorithm first obtains coarse estimates of clock
biases, frequency biases, and the position and velocity
of the radiation source using the proposed distributed
MPF method. Subsequently, refined estimates are ob-
tained at each sensor through a diffusion-based gradient
method. The proposed distributed MPF achieves global
optimization with a relatively small number of particles by
enabling interactions among PFs within each sensor and
local fusion among neighboring sensors. This provides
reliable initial values for the subsequent fine estimation
stage. Throughout the entire processing, information is
shared via single-hop transmissions between neighboring
sensors and fused locally, making the algorithm well-
suited for large-scale sensor networks. Experimental re-
sults demonstrated that the proposed algorithm achieves
consistent convergence across the entire sensor network
and maintains robustness even under low SNR environ-
ments and limited observation durations.
