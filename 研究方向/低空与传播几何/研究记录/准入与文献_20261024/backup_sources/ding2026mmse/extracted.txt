MMSE-based Distributed Direct Position
Determination in Single-hop Sensor Networks
Bowen Ding, Dan Song, Boyu Han, and Wei Wang
Abstract—Distributed adaptive direct position determination
(D-ADPD) is a time-difference-of-arrival (TDOA)-based localiza-
tion algorithm designed for single-hop sensor networks. The algo-
rithm is iterative and typically initialized by using two-step meth-
ods. However, the obtained initial values and the convergence
performance of the D-ADPD are poor in low signal-to-noise ratio
(SNR) environments, resulting in significant positioning errors.
The algorithm also fails when channel propagation induces phase
offsets. To address these issues, this paper proposes a distributed
direct position determination algorithm with single-hop trans-
mission. The proposed algorithm consists of three steps: First,
a distributed frequency domain detection method is proposed
to identify the radiated signal’s frequency components. A cost
function is then formulated based on these detected frequency
components with the minimum mean square error (MMSE)
criterion to eliminate the influence of out-of-band noise. Second, a
coarse positioning method is introduced to provide reliable initial
values for subsequent iteration. Finally, the algorithm iteratively
estimates target positions and compensates for phase offsets using
the diffusion strategy and the gradient method. Simulation results
demonstrate that the proposed algorithm outperforms the D-
ADPD and other similar methods in low SNR environments.
When the SNR is between -15 dB and 10 dB, the root mean square
error of the proposed positioning algorithm is approximately
5 dB lower than other distributed direct localization methods
and about 10 dB lower than that of the classical distributed two-
step method. Furthermore, it is insensitive to the channel-induced
phase offset, exhibiting greater robustness than the D-ADPD.
Index Terms—Direct position determination (DPD), distributed
localization, wireless sensor networks, single-hop, minimum mean
square error (MMSE), time difference of arrival (TDOA).

as vehicle localization [1], [2], navigation [3], environment

passive technique requires no signal emission, thus lowering
power consumption and system costs.
In recent years, direct position determination (DPD) algo-
rithms [7]–[9] have gained significant attention due to their
higher positioning accuracy in low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
environments compared to traditional two-step methods [10]–
[13]. Moreover, DPD algorithms are capable of localizing time

for two-step methods. Unlike two-step methods that require
estimating the time difference of arrival (TDOA), DPD algo-
rithms directly process the received signals to obtain the target
position in one step.
DPD algorithms can be categorized into centralized DPD al-
gorithms [17]–[19] and distributed DPD algorithms [20]–[22]
based on their signal fusion method. In centralized processing,
each sensor transmits its observed signals to a fusion center for
integrated processing. In contrast, distributed DPD algorithms
operate in a decentralized network where computational tasks
are spread across sensors, enabling each sensor to collabo-
ratively solve the target position. Consequently, distributed
DPD algorithms require lower communication bandwidth and
computing resources compared to centralized DPD algorithms.
Additionally, they mitigate the risk of system failure due to
fusion center malfunction [23]–[25].
Early studies on distributed DPD algorithms focused on
exchanging positioning data through multi-hop transmission
within the sensor network. Sensors store and forward the
received data from their neighbors until it reaches a specific
sensor. To reduce the computational and communication load
of distributed DPD, M. Pourhomayoun et al. [20] proposed
three algorithms. The first reduces computation by using the
Gershgorin theorem to approximate the maximum eigenvalue
of the cross ambiguity matrix (CAM). To lower communi-
cation load, the second algorithm leverages the symmetry of
the cross-ambiguity function (CAF) and image compression,
while the third reconstructs the CAM from partial CAF values
and TDOA measurements. However, all three methods rely on
multi-hop transmission of signals and CAF values, demanding
significant communication bandwidth. The distributed DPD
algorithm proposed in [21] limits the sharing of observed
signals to neighboring sensors only, requiring single-hop trans-
mission of observed signals. Nevertheless, the measured CAF
values still need to be transmitted to every sensor via multi-
hop transmission. Building upon this approach, the iterative
distributed DPD (IDDPD) algorithm proposed in [22] en-
hances the positioning solution method. By incorporating deep
neural networks, the IDDPD algorithm transforms the cost
function into a gradient-solvable form and iteratively solves
for the target position using gradient methods, improving
computational efficiency compared to the original importance
sampling method.

resource requirements, system complexity, and communication
overheads such as latency and packet loss. To overcome

distributed DPD method. In the D-ADPD algorithm, data
transmitted to neighboring sensors does not continue to propa-
gate within the sensor network. Based on the diffusion strategy
[28]–[30], sensors only need to share observed signals and
diffusion information with their neighbors to achieve global
consensus. Experiments have shown that the performance of
the D-ADPD algorithm is superior to the centralized adaptive
direct position determination algorithm (C-ADPD) proposed
in [31]. Despite these advantages, the D-ADPD algorithm
still faces several limitations. First, the initialization of its
iterative process typically relies on two-step methods, which
tend to yield inaccurate estimates in low SNR environments.
Second, the algorithm exhibits poor convergence at low SNRs,
even when initialized with accurate values. Consequently,
these two issues lead to large positioning errors in low SNR
environments. Third, the formulation assumes that path atten-
uation coefficients are real-valued. However, channel-induced
phase offsets are often non-negligible in practical positioning
scenarios, resulting in complex-valued attenuation coefficients.
This mismatch renders the D-ADPD algorithm ineffective in
such realistic environments.
In addition to the D-ADPD algorithm, several TDOA-
based distributed localization algorithms have been devel-
oped for single-hop sensor networks. The distributed two-
step method proposed in [32] reformulates emitter localization
from local TDOA measurements as an optimization problem,
incorporating constraints to ensure consistency across sensor
estimates. To further address estimation consistency, diffusion
strategies were integrated into the two-step framework in [33]
and [34]. These algorithms achieve comparable localization
performance to the classical centralized two-step method.
In contrast to the aforementioned methods that rely on the
nonlinear TDOA model, [35] introduced a distributed two-
step algorithm based on a linear TDOA model, which fur-
ther improves localization accuracy. However, obtaining linear
TDOA measurements requires each sensor to estimate the time
of arrival (TOA) of the emitter signal, making the approach
unsuitable for non-cooperative emitter localization. Moreover,
these distributed two-step methods [32]–[35] require the es-
timation of TOA/TDOA in their initial step, which inher-
ently introduces information loss. Consequently, they generally
exhibit lower localization accuracy compared to distributed
direct localization methods, especially in low SNR regimes.
The distributed direct localization method proposed in [36]
constructs a local cost function, which is defined as the sum
of the magnitudes of the CAFs between a sensor and its
neighboring sensors. However, as an iterative algorithm, it
does not address the issue of obtaining reliable initial value,
which can lead to the problem of being trapped in local optima
when the initial value is inaccurate. In summary, existing
distributed localization algorithms fall short of fully resolving
the limitations associated with D-ADPD.
Motivated by these challenges, this paper proposes a dis-
tributed DPD algorithm designed for single-hop sensor net-
works. A cost function is constructed in the frequency domain
based on the minimum mean square error (MMSE) criterion.
The proposed positioning algorithm comprises three steps:
frequency domain detection, coarse positioning, and fine posi-
tioning. By introducing a distributed frequency domain detec-
tion method, our positioning algorithm selectively processes
only the detected frequency components, thereby reducing
computational load and improving positioning accuracy. In
both coarse and fine positioning steps, the algorithm eliminates
the need to estimate TDOAs and obtains position estimates
directly through signal processing. By fully utilizing prior
information from the received signals, the proposed coarse
positioning method achieves high positioning accuracy even in
low SNR environments, providing reliable initial values for the
fine positioning step. Additionally, the algorithm incorporates
a phase term into the cost function to compensate for phase dif-
ferences caused by different channels. In the fine positioning
step, the algorithm iteratively estimates both the target position
and the phase compensation terms, making it applicable to
scenarios involving both real-valued and complex-valued path
attenuation coefficients.
The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
1) A new cost function for single-hop distributed DPD
algorithms is constructed. This cost function is computed
in the signal’s frequency domain, thereby avoiding the
errors introduced by the sine cardinal interpolation used
in the time-domain cost function calculation. Further-
more, a phase term is incorporated into the cost function
to compensate for the channel-induced phase shift, en-
abling its effective application in scenarios with complex
path attenuation coefficients.
2) Based on the constructed cost function, a distributed
DPD method is proposed, which comprises frequency-
domain detection, coarse positioning, and fine position-
ing stages. The proposed algorithm is fully distributed,
requiring information exchange only between neigh-
boring sensors without the need for data forwarding
or global information fusion. Additionally, its iterative
mechanism is well-suited for pipeline processing. These
features make the algorithm particularly suitable for
resource-constrained sensor networks.
3) The coarse positioning method integrates distributed
update operations with a ring neighborhood topology,
providing reliable initial estimates for iterative process-
ing even under low SNR conditions and in multi-emitter
environments.
This paper is organized as follows. Section II introduces the
distributed positioning scenario and signal model. Section III
presents the distributed frequency domain detection method.
Section IV introduces the proposed distributed DPD algorithm.
Section V shows the simulation results. Finally, Section VI
concludes this paper.

II. MODELING OF THE PROBLEM
This section first establishes the model of the distributed
positioning problem and introduces the relevant concepts of
the neighborhood. Subsequently, the signals received by each
sensor are modeled, providing the theoretical foundation for
the proposed positioning algorithm.
A. Distributed Positioning Scenario
The positioning scenario is simplified by assuming a single
emitter in the space. To determine its position u, a synchro-
nized sensor network consisting of M sensors is deployed.
The set V = {1, 2, ..., M} denotes the deployed sensors in
the network. Each sensor i can receive the signals emitted by
the target and obtain its own position pi, i ∈V .
While processing the received signals, each sensor can
share data related to the target and itself with the other
sensors to achieve collaborative positioning. Due to limitations
in communication resources and energy consumption, it is
assumed that sensors only communicate with their neighbors.
Specifically, sensors have a communication radius within
which other sensors are considered their neighbors. The set of
all communication edges is denoted as E. If the sensor i and
the sensor m are neighbors, then (i, m) ∈E ⇐⇒(m, i) ∈E.
All neighbors of the sensor i form its neighborhood
Ni = {m|m ∈V, (i, m) ∈E} = {i1, i2, · · · , i|Ni|},

where |Ni| represents the number of the i-th sensor’s neigh-
bors. Fig. 1 shows a scenario in which a sensor network with
ten sensors is used to locate an emitter. In the figure, the
dashed and solid lines represent the communication range and
communication links, respectively.

DPD algorithms start by establishing a cost function, where
the extremum of the cost function corresponds to the estimated
target position. To find the extremum, iterative DPD algorithms
often require an accurate coarse positioning result for proper
initialization to avoid convergence to local optima. Therefore,
this section presents the proposed distributed DPD algorithm,
sequentially describing its cost function, coarse positioning
method, and fine positioning method.

extensive historical data storage and complex computations.
This makes the algorithm particularly compatible with the low-
power processors commonly found in sensor networks.
V. SIMULATION RESULTS
A. Simulation Setup
To locate the emitter, ten sensors are deployed in a 4000 m
× 4000 m area, with their positions consistent with those in
[21] and listed in Table II. The area of interest for positioning
is the central 2000 m × 2000 m region. Each sensor has a
communication radius of R = 1800 m and communicates only
with sensors within this range.
Two emitters are placed at positions [0m, 200m]T and
[500m, 200m]T. Each emitter radiates a linear frequency mod-
ulation (LFM) signal with a bandwidth of 2 MHz, a pulse
width of 0.4 ms, and an average power of σ2
s = 100 W. The
signals received by sensors overlap in the time domain but
differ in center frequencies. Except for the coarse localization
experiment in Section V-E and the multi-target localization
experiment in Section V-F where both emitters are used, all
other experiments consider single-emitter scenarios that retain
only the first emitter. Unless otherwise specified, the path
attenuation coefficients are set to η1 = η2 = · · · = ηM = 1.
The Monte Carlo simulation is repeated Nexp = 1000 times.
The frequency domain block size is L = 3000. Each sensor
has Np = 10 particles. The numbers of iterations for coarse
and fine positioning are set to tmax = 50 and Niter = 1000,
respectively. The DTV-Burg method uses N = 4000 samples
for frequency domain detection. The clustering method used
in coarse positioning is density based spatial clustering of
applications with noise (DBSCAN) [43], with a distance
parameter epsilon = 100 m and a minimum of 15 data points
required to form a core point. The distance threshold is set
to Th = 100 m. The step sizes for emitter position and phase
updates are µi = 0.5 and βi = 0.0005, respectively.

In this subsection, the proposed algorithm is compared with
several representative TDOA-based positioning algorithms.
The benchmark algorithms include the distributed DPD al-
gorithm from [21], the D-ADPD algorithm from [27], the D-
MCDPD algorithm from [36], the two-step method from [14],
and the distributed two-step method from [32]. Among them,
D-MCDPD, D-ADPD and method from [21] are distributed
DPD algorithms. The distributed DPD [21] algorithm and the
two-step method require multi-hop transmission, while the
other algorithms only necessitate single-hop transmission. In
both the two-step method and the distributed two-step method,
TDOA parameters are obtained through exhaustive search for
the maximum of the CAF. For all algorithms, each sensor
collects 0.4 ms of signal, corresponding to 4000 samples. In
the distributed two-step method, the localization problem is
solved iteratively using the alternating direction method of
multipliers (ADMM).

VI. CONCLUSION
This paper proposed a distributed direct position determina-
tion algorithm designed for single-hop sensor networks. The
algorithm consists of three steps: frequency domain detection,
coarse positioning, and fine positioning. The frequency domain
detection step identifies the frequency components of the radi-
ated signal. In the next two steps, the algorithm processes only
the detected frequency components to reduce computational
load and mitigate the impact of out-of-band noise, thereby
enhancing positioning accuracy. The coarse positioning step
provides reliable initial values even in low SNR environments.
During the fine positioning step, the algorithm iteratively
estimates both the target positions and the phase compensation
terms, eliminating the impact of signal phase shifts caused by
channel propagation. Simulation results demonstrate that in
low SNR environments, the proposed algorithm superior po-
sitioning accuracy compared with the D-ADPD algorithm and
other representative TDOA-based methods. Furthermore, the
proposed algorithm can converge in scenarios with complex
path attenuation coefficients, making it more robust compared
to the D-ADPD algorithm.
