Location Estimates From Channel State Information
via Binary Programming
Muhammed Tahsin Rahman
, Graduate Student Member, IEEE, and Shahrokh Valaee
, Fellow, IEEE
Abstract—Recent years have witnessed a marked increase in the
efﬁcacy and speed to solution of binary program solvers. Leverag-
ingthesedevelopments,weproposeconvertingthesparsemodelling
problem to a binary program, and we derive the conditions under
which such a conversion yields the optimal signal support. Further-
more, we derive an upper bound on this condition, which we can
minimize by designing a structured tight frame as the dictionary.
We apply these theoretical insights to binary optimization of angle
of arrival and time of ﬂight estimation (BOAT) using channel
state information (CSI), with the goal of localizing a transmitter
in an indoor multipath-rich environment. Finally, we show how
a covariance array processing approach, together with the novel
binary programming paradigm, results in a system that outper-
forms two state-of-the-art solutions. Our claims are substantiated
using both simulated data and experimental data collected using
“off-the-shelf” WiFi IEEE 802.11ac routers.
Index Terms—Binary program, sparsity, compressed sensing,
angle of arrival estimation, channel state information.
I. INTRODUCTION
T
HE inverse problem may be stated as follows: a sensor
system reports an observation, which is modelled as the
weighted combination of several primary “atoms”. The goal is to
disentangle the atoms that formed the observation, a task that is
increasingly difﬁcult when the number of observations is small.
Notice that the weights by which to combine the atoms tend
to act like nuisance parameters; they make the problem more
difﬁcult, but they can easily be determined once the atoms
themselves are found. This fact motivates our entire approach.
In an under-determined linear system, the atoms are vectors
and the dictionary of atoms is a collection of these vectors
stacked column-wise. Our focus will be on such systems that
exhibit a degree of sparsity in the number of constituent vectors.
It has been shown that these vectors can be recovered optimally
under the compressed sensing (CS) framework. The best known
measure for quantifying such a notion of sparsity is the ℓ0-norm,
which counts the number of non-zero components. Minimizing
this quantity is a form of regularization that penalizes non-
parsimonious estimates.

However, such an optimization problem is combinatorial in
nature and a wealth of literature has focused on relaxing the
ℓ0-norm using ℓp-norm regularization (0 < p ≤1). The most
interesting case, termed Basis Pursuit (BP), is when p = 1 as
the optimization problem becomes convex. Contrary to what we
shall develop, BP puts greater emphasis on the weights than on
explicitly selecting the constituent vectors.
We will discuss a particularly promising application of the
CS framework, namely integrated sensing and communications
(ISAC). In this paper, we consider localizing a transmitter using
channel state information (CSI), which is a signal that is essential
for recovering symbols in a multipath-rich environment. In an
orthogonal frequency division multiplexing (OFDM) system,
CSI represents the complex signal attenuation between each pair
of transmit and receive antennas at each subcarrier frequency.
The phase shifts of CSI components can be parametrized by
linear combinations of angle of arrival (AoA) and time of ﬂight
(ToF) steering vectors. To recover the AoA and ToF from this
parametrization, one needs to solve an inverse problem.
A. Contributions and Organization
In this paper, we fully develop preliminary ideas that we ex-
plored in a previous conference paper [1]. A central contribution
of this work is to show a counter-intuitive, but highly effective,
system: we further constrain the inverse sparse modelling prob-
lem to leverage the efﬁcacy of recent solvers. Instead of relaxing
the problem and exposing the method to resulting inaccuracies,
we show that an appropriate constraint will guarantee an optimal
solution.
In particular, (i) we give a complete exposition of the con-
ditions under which a binary program will recover the signal
support of the sparsity-regularized inverse problem; (ii) we
design a covariance array processing system that satisﬁes the
derived constraints, allowing for binary optimization of AoA
and ToF (BOAT) parameters; (iii) we study system performance
in a variety of simulated scenarios and using real experimental
data collected from commercially available routers.

the dictionary such that binary programming for the inverse
problem is optimal. These insights guide the development of a
localization system based on CSI data. The focus of Section IV
is the dictionary’s spectrum and structure, the cost function,

II. RELATED WORK
To select from the inﬁnite solutions to an underdetermined
system requires knowledge from outside the system. It has been
observed that many signals display sparsity in some domain,
and incorporating this notion of sparsity has been an active area
of research for the past few decades. Early on, the Tikhonov
regularizer [2] was developed that preferred solutions with a
sparse energy proﬁle or those with better smoothness properties.
This technique and subsequent developments proved useful in
recovering images from noise [3], solving integral equations [4],
and in support vector machines [5]. It has permeated even deeper
into current machine learning practices in the form of weight
decay regularization. On the other hand, our understanding of
compression algorithms suggests a different form of sparsity,
one that exists in the signal support rather than the power. Some
researches, such as Donoho, Candès, Tao, and Romberg [6], [7],
further explored this deceptively simple concept, and they have
shown that the problem is quite intricate and nuanced. Named
compressed sensing (CS), it is the focus of our work, and further
references to sparsity will be in this context.
After Chen, Donoho, and Saunders [8] formulated the basis
pursuit problem, replacing the ℓ0-norm with the ℓ1-norm in
(1), the restricted isometry property (RIP) surfaced as a key
property of the dictionary Φ that ensured exact recovery [9]. By
considering the sparsity of x and the isometry constant of Φ,
one can determine whether the original problem or its convex
relaxation will result in exact signal recovery. A great deal
of effort has gone into constructing such dictionaries, whether
deterministically [10] or otherwise [11]. Deﬁnitions such as the
matrix spark and coherence are naturally related to the RIP and
serve as windows to how and why CS works. In this paper,
we will attempt headway to an additional constraint on x under
which the binary program of (2) will also result in exact recovery.
It is worth noting that the sparse modelling paradigm can be
rendered “off-grid” [12]. The key is to select entities from some
sparse representation, whether that representation is over canon-
ical basis vectors, unit-norm rank one matrices [13], atoms [14],
etc. Based on the formulation, different characterizations and
recovery solutions emerge.
Far from being relegated to the annals of theory, CS has seen
widespread use in a range of applications. One of the earliest is
in magnetic resonance image (MRI) reconstruction, which has
now been adopted in FDA-approved imaging protocols [15].
Another is in outlier detection, where a subset of the data
that best conforms to a pre-existing model is selected from
the entire dataset [16]. Perhaps the most striking application
is its appearance as dropout regularization and pruning in neural
networks [17]; in a similar vein to what we are proposing,
Salehinejad and Valaee [18] implemented a binary program to
quantify a notion of network energy and select the best nodes or
edges to drop based on the change in network energy.
Directly relevant to our work is the application of CS to
the age-old problem of source localization [8]. Spaced appro-
priately, an array of receiver elements capture a narrowband
signal generated by a source of unknown location. Methods
for recovering the signal’s AoA abound in the literature, but
one of the foundational works is the MUSIC algorithm [19],
which is a subspace-based method that requires computing
the covariance matrix of the data. AoA estimation in the CS
paradigm [20] works on the observations themselves rather
than the covariance matrix, circumventing the need for a large
number of observations. To deal with wideband CSI data, the

It is worth recounting that most current applications of
sparsity-based signal recovery rely on the ℓ1-norm, which
serves to relax the ℓ0-norm. This is necessary for computational
tractability. Bourguignon et al. [23] have pointedly revisited this
taken-for-granted fact, and they showed that a regularized un-
derdetermined system can be cast as a multi object optimization
problem. To this end, they proposed a mixed-integer quadratic
program (MIQP). Two years later the MIQP computational
efﬁciency was improved [24] using the alternating direction
method instead of the branch-and-cut method as implemented
in the commercial CPLEX solver [25].
Indeed, it was previously observed by Blumensath and
Davies [26], and later by Dong and Zhu [27], that sparse signal
estimation was more accurate when the ℓ0-norm was considered
directly. They approximated the solution to the ℓ0-norm using
a suboptimal approach called iterative hard thresholding (ITH).
Karahano˘glu, Erdo˘gan, and Birbil [28] made use of this fact
to develop a mixed-integer linear program (MILP) for sparse
signal recovery; however, their formulation introduced auxiliary
variables and needed apriori knowledge of the range of signal
amplitudes. Mazumder and Radchenko [29] studied the Discrete
Dantzig Selector as another MILP formulation without these
drawbacks. They replaced the ℓ2-norm data ﬁdelity constraint
with the ℓ∞-norm constraint to handle problems with upto
10,000 variables, and they concluded that the improved com-
putational efﬁciency and solution accuracy owed to the maturity
of MILP solvers over MIQP solvers.
This is our point of departure, where we seek instead a binary
programming framework for sparse signal recovery. An advan-
tage of such a framework is that it allows one to conceptualize the
problem in terms of the well known Ising model [30]. The Ising
model is a quadratic energy-based model for binary variables
and emerges naturally in many physical systems. Recent years
have witnessed a reinvigoration of interest in solving binary
programs in general, and the Ising model in particular: extending
the oft-used notion of simulated annealing [31] is the quantum
annealer [32], which uses quantum ﬂuctuation-based computa-
tions to tackle the Ising energy Hamiltonian, with the speculation
of a signiﬁcant speed up. This quantum speed up has yet to be
conclusively demonstrated [33], [34], and other technologies are
surfacing such as the Digital Annealer (DA) [35], which uses
a parallel architecture to implement the Markov Chain Monte
Carlo (MCMC) search algorithm. With such solutions increas-
ingly in the mainstream, converting (1) to a binary program
is bound to yield more accurate and computationally efﬁcient
answers.

smallest number of linearly dependant columns of the matrix.

Indeed, if Lemma 1 is not satisﬁed, then there is no unique so-

sparsity proﬁle. In other words, we cannot distinguish between
the inﬁnitely many solutions. Computing the matrix spark is
itself combinatorial, and one usually relies on bounds through
mutual coherence. Nevertheless, in Section IV, we shall seek
to increase the number of rows M of the dictionary through a
vectorized covariance approach so that the sparsity requirement
may be met more easily.

future work.

IV. SYSTEM DESIGN
We now focus on using OFDM-based CSI data. We will
restrict our discussion to the WiFi IEEE 802.11ac standard,
where a multi antenna receiver operates over several narrowband
frequencies, termed subcarriers.
Across a uniform linear antenna array (ULA) with Na an-
tennas, the phase of a signal incoming at an angle of θ can
be modelled using the steering vector α(θ) = [αi(θ)] ∈CNa
containing elements
αi(θ) = exp(−jkd(i −1) sin(θ)),
(14)
where k is the wave number and d is the antenna spacing. The
overcomplete representation over a grid of Nθ possible AoA is
denoted as A = [αi(θj)] ∈CNa×Nθ.
Across Nf subcarriers spaced at fδ, a signal incoming with a
ToF τ can be modelled using the steering vector β = [βi(τ)] ∈
CNf containing elements
βi(τ) = exp(−j2πfδ(i −1)τ).
(15)
Similar to the AoA grid, the overcomplete representation for a
grid of Nτ possible ToF is B = [βi(τj)] ∈CNf ×Nτ .
For a point source emitter at an AoA-ToF tuple (θ, τ), the
observed CSI vector can be modelled by the Kronecker product,
denoted by ⊗, of (14) and (15). The resulting dictionary over
the set L of (θ, τ) grid points is given by
Φ = A ⊗B ∈CNaNf ×NθNτ .
(16)
Each vector of the dictionary Φ is φ(θ, τ) = α(θ) ⊗β(τ).
Finally, the number of vectors in Φ corresponds to the number
of grid points |L| = N = NθNτ.
An observation at the receiver y = Φx is in the form man-
dated by (1). Solving this would lead to the true signal emitter
locations Ls. As assumed in Theorem 3, Ls ⊂L, such that
|Ls| = L. The points in Ls simply correspond to the non-zero
components of x. In [22], the authors apply the basis pursuit al-
gorithm to this formulation. In our case, converting this problem
to a binary program (2) presents two challenges: (i) under this
model, x refers to the signal amplitudes, which are complex and
do not satisfy Theorem 1; and (ii) the dictionary created from
an arbitrary set L will not satisfy Theorem 4, in general.

V. PERFORMANCE VALIDATION
A major contribution that brought AoA estimation using
CSI to commercial “off-the-shelf” devices was the work by
Kotaru et. al. [21]. Two deﬁning characteristics of their work
were (i) the introduction of ToF as a parameter to improve the
estimation resolution and (ii) the development of a CSI smooth-
ing method to restore the rank of the signal covariance matrix in
a coherent environment, such as indoors. They leveraged these
two ideas and implemented multi signal classiﬁcation (MUSIC)
to resolve a large number of signals in a multipath-rich indoor
environment. Their approach is termed ‘SpotFi,’ and it is the
ﬁrst algorithm to which we shall compare our designed system.
The second work that we shall use as a benchmark was
completed by Gong and Liu, which they termed ‘RoArray’ [22].
Their main contribution was to leverage the sparse modelling

A. Receiver’s Physical Characteristics
We will use the ASUS RT-AC86 U router, which comes
equipped with three antennas and the capability to run IEEE
802.11ac MU-MIMO. This choice is mainly due to the Nexmon
ﬁrmware [53], that enables the router to push CSI data to
userspace, where it can be easily accessed and stored as packet
capture (pcap) ﬁles. We use the Raspberry Pi4 to send ICMP
ping packets at 100 Hz to a nearby device, and conﬁgure the
ASUS RT-AC86 U router to ﬁlter the appropriate channel and
MAC address. In this way, the router acts as a receiver.
It is important to mention that the router antenna spacing is
8.7 cm. For channel 1 centred at 2.412 GHz, the ‘ideal’ and
mostfrequentlyassumedspacingisthehalf-wavelengthspacing,
which is 6.2 cm. This is because, at half-wavelength spacing,
there is no phase ambiguity for θ ∈[−90◦, 90◦] owing to the
structure of steering vectors (14). On the other hand, a larger
spacing improves the sensitivity of the system to small variations
in θ. This can be veriﬁed by considering Fig. 3(a): as the spacing
increases from quarter-wavelength to half-wavelength the slope
of the phase difference versus AoA curve increases, which leads
to a larger observed phase difference for the same change in
AoA. Effectively, larger spacing leads to better signal-to-noise
ratio (SNR). Due to the physical limitation of the device we use,
to avoid phase ambiguities we shall limit the ground truth AoA
that we consider to the range [−24◦, 24◦], and we limit the range
of the AoA grid θ1, θ2, . . . , θNθ each to [−45◦, 45◦].
Although the antenna spacing is ﬁxed, we have more ﬂex-
ibility in selecting which subcarriers to use. For 20 MHz of
bandwidth, 64 subcarriers are considered by the transceivers for
the FFT operation, out of which 52 are actively utilized [54].
Adjacent subcarriers are spaced at 312.5 kHz. Analogous to
antenna spacing, utilizing closely spaced subcarriers will in-
crease the range at which phase wrapping occurs in the steering
vectors (15), but it will reduce the slope of the phase difference
versus ToF curve, thereby reducing the SNR. This is illustrated
in Fig. 3(b). We will assume that the expected range of our WiFi
router is about 30 m. So, to improve SNR, we select the maxi-
mum subcarrier spacing fδ such that the phase wrap occurs at or
beyond 35 m, allowing us to grid the space in the range [0, 35].
This corresponds to 8.4375 MHz spacing, which amounts to 27
subcarriers. For example, CSI of subcarrier number −26 and
subcarrier number 1 are used. The rest of the subcarriers’ CSI
are used in the smoothing procedure to restore the rank of the
covariancematrix.WhileNf = 2mayseemlikeasmallnumber,
from Theorem 5 and (19) the effective length of an observation
vector using our system is (2Na −1)(2Nf −1) = 15, which is
considerable.
Finally, the receiver and transmitter are not synchronized, and
several deleterious effects appear in the CSI phase, such as signal
timing offset (STO), sampling frequency offset (SFO), center
frequency offset (CFO), etc [21], [55]. These non-idealities may
be compensated for by removing the linear component of the
phase across subcarriers for each antenna. Doing so introduces a
large unknown bias to the ToF estimates, hampering a complete
localization system using a single router. This bias does not
appear in the AoA estimates, however, since they rely on the
phase difference between antennas, which subtracts out the
phase contamination. In our simulation results we are able to
recover accurate AoA and ToF estimates, but using experimental
data we shall rely only on the AoA estimates.

for low noise scenarios, the BOAT-MP algorithm’s performance
did not converge to a Jaccard index of 1. Nevertheless, sub-meter
accuracy was achieved by all the BOAT solvers at and above
16 dB SNR.
2) No. of Packets: Next, we compare performance for 20 dB
SNR using different number of packets to generate the co-
variance matrix. Our results for the angle estimation error and
localization error are shown in Fig. 5(a) and (b), respectively,
while the Jaccard index is shown in Fig. 5(c).
For a small number of packets, the BOAT-MP method has
the lowest error and achieves sub-meter accuracy using only
20 packets. The Gurobi solver performs better than the DA
when using 5 and 10 packets; however, using 30 packets or
more, the DA’s performance is comparable to the BOAT-MP
algorithm’s performance. Then, at 50 packets, the Gurobi solver
matches the DA. Similar to the case with varying SNR, the
BOAT-MP algorithm does not converge to a Jaccard coefﬁcient
of 1, even when using a large number of packets. The SpotFi
algorithm’s accuracy does not signiﬁcantly increase as do the
BOAT solvers when large number of packets are used, even
though both systems rely on covariance matrix computations.
Both Gurobi and the DA have very similar performance when
it comes to estimating the angles; the differences are more
pronounced when ToF is taken into account for localization.
In any case, it is clear that the solvers perform differently under
different circumstances. This is promising because it suggests
that as solvers get more accurate, then so too will BOAT’s
performance.
C. Experimental Results
We setup the ASUS router and Raspberry Pi4 10 m apart in
a 3 m wide corridor. The devices were placed on two stands,
1.2 m high, and for each of the angular offsets the router was
turned in place and CSI data was collected for 5 minutes. The
transmitter was set to ping at 100 Hz, so this amounted to about
30,000 packets at each angular offset. The actual number was
slightly lower due to packet loss. We performed AoA and ToF
estimation at a rate of 1 estimate per second, which was about
80-100 packets of CSI for computing the covariance matrix.
The corridor had low foot trafﬁc throughout the experiment and
the line-of-sight (LoS) between transmitter and receiver was not
blocked. Since the ToF estimates have an inherent bias due to
the hardware effects, we shall only report the AoA estimates.
Note that the Pi4 did not lie on the designed equivalence grid.

D. Timing
We have measured the time to solution for each of the meth-
ods, presented in Table I. As the solution by Gurobi was initially
taking too long we had to limit the time to 30 s. No other
estimator was time limited. Being a simple greedy algorithm
with linear time complexity O(LN), the BOAT-MP method
was signiﬁcantly faster than other methods. Interestingly, it is
an order of magnitude faster than the OMP algorithm, which
needs to compute the optimal scaling at each iteration. The DA
was faster than both the Gurobi and, surprisingly, the ℓ1-norm
SOCP. SpotFi, based on a 2D exhaustive search over the pseudo
spectrum, was the second fastest estimator.
VI. CONCLUSION
We have explored solving the sparse inverse problem using
a binary programming approach, termed BOAT. The key devel-
opments of this paper were the conditions under which such
a program recovers the signal support, the design of a system
using these conditions, and a thorough evaluation of the sys-
tem performance using both simulated and experimental data.
Our application domain was localization using channel state
information (CSI) data that is needed for symbol recovery in an
OFDM-based communication system such as WiFi. Compared
to two state-of-the-art solutions in this area, our system shows
an improvement in both the localization accuracy and speed to
solution.

Proof of Theorem 5. We have the following three properties
regarding the Kronecker product and the Khatri-Rao product.
P1: For matrices C ∈Cm×n, D ∈Cp×q, E ∈Cr×n, F ∈
Ck×q, (C ⊗D) ⊙(E ⊗F ) is permutationally equivalent to
(C ⊙E) ⊗(D ⊙F ). This is similar to the mixed-product
property [43], except that the two expression are equivalent
only upto a reordering of the rows. This property can be readily
veriﬁed by writing out the partitioned multiplications.
P2 [43]: For C ∈Cm×n and D ∈Cp×q,
rank(C ⊗D) = rank(C) rank(D).
P3 [45]: For a Vandermonde matrix V ∈Cn×k,
rank(V ∗⊙V ) = min{2n −1, k}.
Based on the AoA grid A and ToF grid B, and because
equivalence upto a permutation guarantees rank preservation,
we may apply P1 and P2 to write
rank(Φ∗⊙Φ) = rank((A∗⊗B∗) ⊙(A ⊗B))
= rank((A∗⊙A) ⊗(B∗⊙B))
= rank(A∗⊙A) rank(B∗⊙B).
Both A and B are overcomplete Vandermonde matrices, with
respectively Na and Nf rows. k is large because the dictionaries
are overcomplete, so from P3
rank(A∗⊙A) rank(B∗⊙B) = (2Na −1)(2Nf −1).
