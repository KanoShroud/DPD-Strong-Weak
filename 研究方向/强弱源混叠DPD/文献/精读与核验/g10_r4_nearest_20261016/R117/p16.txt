5.2. Cases of Gaussian pulse acoustic sources identification

   To prove the effectiveness of the proposed method for transient source identification, in this section, we perform Gaussian pulse
source identification and compare the source identification performance of these four methods. We assume that there are three
Gaussian pulse sources S1, S2, and S3 located at ( −0.04, −0.2), (0.08, −0.2), and (−0.2, 0.32) m, respectively, all of them have a
half-peak bandwidth of 0.0001 s, and the peak times of each source are 0.001 s apart. In this simulation, the settings of the array,
measurement distance and discretized grid are the same as those in Section 5.1. Fast Fourier transform (FFT) is performed on the sound
pressure signal of each microphone of the array to obtain the Fourier spectrum of the signal, where the sampling frequency is 102,400
Hz and the frequency resolution (sub-band width) is 50 Hz. Fig. 8 shows the source identification results for 81 sub-bands from 1000
Hz to 5000 Hz. The SNR at each frequency is set to 20 dB. Fig. 9 shows the results with SNR of 5 dB. In both sets of results, subplots (a)-
(d) are the localization colormaps of each method, and subplots (e)-(h) are the source strength quantification results of all sources at
each frequency.
   For source S3, as shown in Fig. 8(a)-(d): OMP-CB has scattered localization at different frequencies with the lowest localization
accuracy; NOMP-CB has more concentrated localization at all frequencies, and the localization accuracy is relatively high; BOMP-CB
localizes the source to a nearby grid point, i.e. its localization accuracy is restrained by basis mismatch, and BNOMP-CB obtains the
highest localization accuracy. The source strength quantification curves in Fig. 8(e)-(h) shows that all four methods can accurately
quantify the source strength of S3 at each frequency. For S1 and S2 with small spacing, OMP-CB correctly quantifies the source
strength only at frequencies above 4500 Hz, which is related to the fact that OMP-CB can separate them only when the frequency is
above 4500 Hz. NOMP-CB can separate and accurately quantify the two sources at frequencies above 2500 Hz. BOMP-CB cannot obtain
accurate source strength quantification for both two sources in 1000–5000 Hz. This is because it fails to separate them (as shown in
Fig. 8(c)). BNOMP-CB achieves accurate localization and source strength estimates for them in the full frequency band of interest
1000–5000 Hz. In summary, BNOMP-CB has a much higher spatial resolution than the other three methods and has a good perfor­
mance on transient source identification. It can accurately locate the sources and quantify their strength of each frequency.
   Comparing the identification results of 5 dB SNR in Fig. 9 with those of 20 dB SNR in Fig. 8, it can be seen that as SNR decreases, the
localization results of OMP-CB and NOMP-CB at different frequencies become more scattered, while the localization results of BOMP-
CB and BNOMP-CB keep unchanged or change little. Besides, the source strength quantification accuracy of all four methods is
decreased due to the strong noise. From the location and quantification results of S1 and S2, we can see that when SNR is 5 dB, NOMP-
CB still fails to separate these two closely-spaced sources even when the frequency is higher than 2500 Hz. It is only at frequencies close
to 4500 Hz, NOMP-CB obtains a comparatively accurate source strength quantification. Its quantification curve shows an oscillation
pattern similar to that of OMP-CB. This phenomenon indicates that strong noise interference degrades the spatial resolution of NOMP-
CB. The quantification results of BOMP-CB and BNOMP-CB with SNR of 5 dB remain similar to those with SNR of 20 dB, except for
increased fluctuations, which indicates that the introduction of multi-frequency synchronous processing makes their source identi­
fication performance robust to the noise interference. Therefore, the proposed BNOMP-CB performs well even at low SNR.
