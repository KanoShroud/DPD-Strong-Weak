[PDF p.1] Abstract—To address the performance degradation caused by grid mismatch and unknown channel attenuation, we propose a time difference of arrival (TDOA)-based direct position determination (DPD) method with parametric dictionary learning. First, the off-grid error and the model parameter error are uniformly modeled as the dictionary offset problem. Subsequently, the DPD problem under dictionary perturbation is transformed into a joint optimization problem involving parametric dictionary learning and sparse signal reconstruction, which is solved within the sparse Bayesian learning (SBL) framework. Furthermore, the Cramér-Rao bounds (CRB) for TDOA-based DPD are derived, serving as a benchmark for performance evaluation. Simulation results demonstrate that the proposed method effectively localizes off-grid sources under unknown channel attenuation. The positioning accuracy can approach the CRB under high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and can be improved by over 20% compared with the existing methods under low SNR.

For scenarios where receiving stations employ single antennas and remain relatively stationary to the source, Naresh and Kay proposed a maximum likelihood (ML) based DPD method that relies solely on TDOA information [8]. This approach establishes a cost function for each grid point according to the ML criterion, with the grid point yielding the maximum value identified as the estimated source position. Existing passive positioning systems predominantly adopt a two-step localization approach. First, positioning parameters are estimated from raw signals, such as direction of arrival (DOA) [3], time difference of arrival (TDOA) [4], and frequency difference of arrival (FDOA) [5]. Then, the position of the target is further determined based on these parameters. This two-step method does not require transmitting all signal data to a central processing station, thereby relaxing demands on communication bandwidth and computational load. However, the two-step approach also has several limitations.

However, the successful reconstruction of signals using these methods hinges on the precise alignment between the positioning dictionary and the true signal propagation model. References [15], [16] investigated localization scenarios where radiation sources are precisely located on predefined grid points, ensuring a matched state between the signal and the dictionary. In practical scenarios, it is difficult to obtain a completely accurate positioning dictionary, leading to a basis mismatch within the dictionary matrix. There are two main causes of basis mismatch in DPD. On one hand, the actual target often does not lie exactly on the predefined grid points, resulting in off-grid errors. On the other hand, the signal propagation model may be partially unknown; for example, when affected by unknown channel attenuation, the directly constructed sparse dictionary will also be partially unknown, thereby causing model parameter errors. In sparse reconstruction, the dictionary matrix should be known and accurate; otherwise, the reconstruction accuracy will be reduced due to basis mismatch.

However, the coupling of dictionary and signal uncertainties in the observed data prevents the algorithm from guaranteeing a unique and correct solution. In Reference [23], for scenarios with unknown gain and phase errors, the position parameters were decoupled from the gain-phase errors, rendering the dictionary fully known without requiring learning. Nevertheless, the accuracy of this method entirely depends on the effectiveness of the decoupling, resulting in limited robustness. Reference [24] introduced a learning rule for the channel attenuation factor (i.e., the unknown part of the dictionary) during the localization process, which treated the unknown dictionary components as parameters to be estimated via SBL and updated the sparse dictionary iteratively. However, this approach does not jointly update the attenuation factor and position parameters, thus constraining its learning capability. Methods addressing channel uncertainty in references [22-24] typically learn attenuation as an additional parameter independent of position estimation. However, they often fail to jointly model off-grid errors, which may cause the learning process to fall into local optima and result in significant performance degradation under high off-grid conditions.
