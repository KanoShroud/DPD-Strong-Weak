Passive localization of overlapping strong and weak
sources from short observations
Abstract
Spectral overlap and unequal received powers complicate weak-source delay estimation and
association across receivers in passive localization. This report studies weak-source localization
and computational cost using direct position determination (DPD), with four synchronized
single-antenna receivers and two sources under line-of-sight propagation.
The source count
is assumed known.
Eliminating the unknown source coeﬀicients at each frequency gives a
concentrated least-squares objective, from which projection loss, strong-source position errors,
and missed candidates are examined. With a 20 m success radius, the isolated weak source is
localized successfully in 90.63% and 95.31% of the 4096- and 16384-sample records, respectively.
Adding a strong source reduces these rates to 33.98% and 38.28% for the standard regional
search. Across 48 conditions in six new layouts, supplementary global search on a 5 m grid
followed by joint updates increases the success count from 8 to 40, while mean total processing
time rises from 8.04 s to 31.10 s.
A regional score upper bound is derived from response
perturbations to reduce search cost. On 24 fixed diagnostic records, subdivision prioritized by
this bound recovers 9 of 16 previously failed cases, compared with 15 for the 5 m scan, and
prunes no cells. Broader candidate coverage improves recovery, but the current bound does not
yet provide comparable recovery at lower cost.

Introduction
1.1
Application context and overlapping sources
In distributed spectrum monitoring and localization of noncooperative emitters, the transmitted
waveforms and transmission times are usually unavailable. Positions must therefore be inferred
from observations at multiple receivers. Several emitters may occupy the same reception band,
so each record contains a mixture of sources. Differences in transmit power and propagation dis-
tance produce unequal received powers. Here, spectral overlap means overlap between frequency
supports; it does not refer to aliasing caused by undersampling.
The setting of interest is a weak source that can be localized in isolation but is often mislo-
calized when a strong source is present. Strong and weak are defined by received power averaged
across the receiver network; the same source need not be stronger at every receiver. With short
observations, fluctuations in sample correlations and cross terms between sources also affect
estimation. Both sources must be localized within a limited processing time, so strong-source
accuracy alone is insuﬀicient to assess performance.
1.2
Diﬀiculties in two-step localization and the DPD approach
Conventional two-step localization first estimates time differences of arrival (TDOAs) from re-
ceiver pairs, then solves for position using the resulting geometric constraints.
Generalized
1


cross-correlation provides a basic framework for delay estimation [1]. The hyperbolic estima-
tor of Chan and Ho and subsequent bias analysis address the position solution given delay
measurements [2], [3].
For a mixture of strong and weak sources, cross-correlation between receivers contains shifted
autocorrelation terms from each source, finite-sample cross terms, and noise. Strong-source
terms may mask the weak-source peak. Even if several peaks are retained, the delays from
different receiver pairs must still be associated with the same source. Selecting the largest peak
independently for each pair can produce delays that are inconsistent with any single position.
These diﬀiculties arise in measurement extraction and association, before the geometric position
solution.
DPD constructs an estimation objective directly from the received signals and the propaga-
tion responses of candidate positions. The observations thus constrain position jointly across
receivers. Weiss and Amar discuss the distinction between direct and two-step localization, in-
cluding the association problem for multiple sources [4]. DPD is used here to avoid committing
prematurely to a single delay peak at the front end. Coupling between sources and the search
over a multimodal objective remain.
1.3
Related work and research objective
Chen et al. formulate maximum-likelihood localization for wideband near-field observations and
handle multiple sources by eliminating unknown source spectra and alternating position updates
[5]. Their formulation supports the joint fitting of unknown frequency-domain coeﬀicients used
here. High-resolution DPD (DPD-HR) uses statistics from segmented observations to construct
a localization score [6]; the observation duration and number of segments required for those
statistics must be compatible with short records.
Sequential suppression and cyclic updates are one approach to interference from unequal
source powers. In acoustic matched-field localization, the round-robin method of Mantzel et al.
projects out estimated sources and uses position revisits and rank-constrained regional suppres-
sion to reduce the effects of estimation errors [7]. This report adopts the projection and revisit
ideas with a line-of-sight radio-frequency response; the acoustic performance results are not
transferred to the present setting. Newtonized orthogonal matching pursuit (NOMP) combines
component detection, continuous refinement, and cyclic updates [8]. Its sinusoidal model differs
from the two-dimensional position model with unknown coeﬀicients at each frequency consid-
ered here.
Multiple-candidate representations also appear in steered response power (SRP)
and group-sparse direction estimation [9], although their dictionaries and geometric constraints
differ from those of localization with distributed receivers.
Joint estimation, projection, and continuous optimization provide a basis for multisource
localization, yet the weak source may still be missed in a short mixed observation. This re-
port uses conditional scores to examine missed candidates and position coupling, compares the
recovery and cost of denser global search, and tests whether a response-based regional upper
bound can reduce score evaluations. The objective is to retain candidates near narrow weak-
source peaks at lower cost and to reduce the effect of strong-source position errors through joint
updates.

3.10
Regional subdivision on fixed diagnostic records
To test whether (2.22) can reduce search effort, four single-update weak-source searches are
compared on the same 24 diagnostic records as in Section 3.6: a uniform 5 m grid; a 10 m grid
shifted by 5 m along each coordinate; score-prioritized subdivision; and upper-bound-prioritized
subdivision. All strategies fix the original strong-source estimate, retain the top 5 candidates,
and use the same continuous refinement. An output is retained only if its score is no lower than
that of the initial estimate. The 5 m scan here updates only the weak position once, unlike the
sequential global searches and joint updates of complete method D.
Both subdivision strategies start from a 50 m grid with initial cell half-width 25 m. They
subdivide at most 32 cells per step, evaluate at most 16384 grid points, and use a minimum
half-width of 1.5625 m. Score-prioritized subdivision ranks cells by center score. Upper-bound-
prioritized subdivision ranks them by U(C) and prunes a cell when its bound is below the best
score found. Numerical checks cover two record lengths, zero to two fixed sources, and four cell
scales, giving 24 configurations. No sampled score exceeds its cell bound. These checks assess
numerical consistency, not localization performance.

3.11
Findings
In the 32-layout comparison, sequential projection and joint updates raise weak-source success
within 20 m to 31% to 36%, still below the isolated-source rates of 91% to 95%. Conditional
scores reveal better positions that the solver misses in most failures. Strong-source position
errors also affect weak-source scores in some records.
Response-variation ranking in C shows no consistent benefit over B. Supplementary 5 m
global search in D increases weak-source successes from 8 of 48 to 40 of 48 in six new layouts,
at 3.87 times the mean total processing time. On the fixed diagnostic sample, the regional
bound improves subdivision order but produces no pruning and recovers fewer failures than the
dense grid. Search design must reduce unproductive evaluations while preserving narrow-peak
candidates. Longer runs or a different ranking alone have not resolved this tradeoff.
All conditions in one layout still fail after the complete dense-search procedure. The single-
update comparisons with a fixed strong estimate do not explain those remaining failures. Candi-
date omissions and position coupling must be separated to determine when both source positions
should change together.
4
Discussion
4.1
Narrow-peak width and search scale
In Table 3.6, the grid point 4.523 m from the true position scores below a distant incorrect peak,
while a local solution near the truth has error 0.803 m. Equation (2.17) describes second-order
behavior near a peak, but neither its location nor its curvature is known before the search. It
therefore does not directly determine grid spacing.
Equation (2.22) provides a regional score bound from response perturbations, yet it yields
no pruning in the present comparison. When the projected-response norm is small or phase
changes substantially across a cell, the directional constraint can reduce to the residual-energy
bound. The remaining question is how to use center scores, response derivatives, and residuals
to tighten the bound at acceptable cost. Equivalent Fisher information describes local statistical
accuracy, whereas the score Hessian describes peak shape for one observation. Conditions under
which either can guide search scale need to be established.
4.2
Candidate selection and computational allocation
In 292 of 327 weak-source failures, the true weak position already scores higher under the
original strong estimate. D recovers many failures on new layouts, but its mean time is 3.87
times that of B. On the fixed records, bound-prioritized subdivision also recovers fewer failures
than the simple shifted grid. A more elaborate cell rule does not necessarily preserve more useful
candidates for a given computational cost. When a cell center has a low score, what observed
information justifies further subdivision? A few additional samples, residual correlations, or
21


curvature estimates may help reduce uniform global refinement, but their cost and recovery
must be assessed together.
Multiple delay hypotheses are another possible source of position candidates. Relative to
receiver 1, position determines the delay difference
∆τℓ1(p) = ∥p −rℓ∥−∥p −r1∥

Several correlation peaks could be retained, geometric consistency used to generate position
candidates, and the joint residual used to select among them [1], [2], [3]. The unresolved choices
are whether to estimate delays from the raw mixture or the residual after strong-source projec-
tion, and when to impose cross-receiver constraints so that incorrect combinations are reduced
without discarding weak-source hypotheses too early.
Correlation computation, hypothesis
combinations, and signal-domain validation must all be included in processing time.
In the same selected cases, increasing the retained candidate count from 5 to 32 on a 10 m
grid gives no additional recovery, whereas a 5 m grid with 5 candidates recovers more records.
Candidate count and sampling density therefore require separate treatment. New selection rules
should retain uniform and shifted grids as references, compare recovery and cost under the same
continuous-refinement configuration, and account for total time in the complete solver.
4.3
Strong-source accuracy and joint position updates
In the conditional-score analysis, substituting the true strong position restores a score advantage
at the true weak position in 22 records, including one whose original strong-source error is below
20 m. Equation (2.16) approximates leakage for small errors, but the true error is unavailable
during estimation, and the success threshold cannot determine when refinement should stop.
The remaining failures of D occur in one layout, raising the question of whether sequential
updates are constrained by coupled local solutions.
Can local curvature, response derivatives, and residuals estimate the benefit of refining the
strong position versus searching again for the weak source? Alternating updates change one
position at a time and may repeatedly return to the same local solution. When to retain multiple
strong-position hypotheses or update all four position coordinates jointly remains unresolved. In
a four-receiver system, expanding the suppression subspace also reduces the retained dimension
[7]. These alternatives therefore need to be compared in terms of strong-source leakage, loss of
weak-source information, and additional computation.
